PA2 — Secondary Research & Source Audit
Points 100 (term-project component)
Due Sunday Sep 13, 11:59 PM
Work Team
Submit Canvas — one PDF (memo, ≤ 6 pages) + the filled Source Audit Form from Appendix A

Where this fits: Lecture — Session 2.1 Secondary Research (week 2) + the librarian guest visit Wednesday Sep 2 → Practice — Summit Point rounds r03 (Neo RFP) and r04 (Analyze secondary research) → Now the real thing: the desk-research memo that precedes the PA3 client interview. Professionals never walk into a client conversation cold.

The gist

Neo Smart Living is launching the Tahoe Mini, a prefab backyard studio at roughly $23,000 installed. Before anyone designs a survey or talks to the founder, your team builds the picture the public record already holds: what the category is, who competes, what substitutes, where $23K sits, and what California lets you put in a backyard. You do it the modern way (AI deep research), the traditional way (a licensed library report), and then run the source audit that decides which claims are safe to put in front of a client.

Steps

  1. Run Part A — AI deep research. Use a deep-research-capable tool with at least two scoped prompts (samples in Appendix B); keep full transcripts and links. Iterate — demand a citation for every claim, and treat every output as unverified draft until Part C.
  2. Write the memo (≤ 6 pages incl. tables): category definition & size (studio pod vs. ADU vs. tiny home vs. shed — and why); a California regulatory snapshot (when a permit is required, what the under-120-sq-ft exemption actually exempts, why "no plumbing" matters); ≥ 4 direct competitors with advertised price and what it excludes; substitutes with at least one price trap; customer evidence (vendor claims vs. independent); and where $23K sits among completed costs, not headlines.
  3. Do Part B — the library pass. Through the CPP Library database portal (not Google), retrieve one licensed industry report — Mintel, IBISWorld, or Statista; ask a librarian if unsure which fits. Extract three findings that appeared nowhere in your AI pass.
  4. Write the AI-vs-library comparison (half a page): what the licensed report had that the open web didn't, where the two disagreed, which you trust for what — plus one line on your search path (databases tried, librarian consulted).
  5. Audit every number — Part C. Fill the Source Audit Form (Appendix A) for ≥ 12 claims, including every number in the memo: publisher type, date, who collected it and why, the method behind the number, corroboration, verdict, action taken.
  6. Push until something breaks. A clean audit is a failed audit: we expect ≥ 2 rows rated Unverified or Contradicted, each with a real action (claim cut, ranged, or downgraded to "vendor-claimed"). If everything verified cleanly, you didn't push hard enough.
  7. Build the interview bridge. Close the memo with five founder questions secondary research cannot answer and three hypotheses for your primary research — each citing the section that motivated it. This is your PA3 interview prep.

What to submit

  • [ ] Secondary research memo (≤ 6 pages): every factual claim cited (source name + link); every less-than-Verified number flagged in the text
  • [ ] Library block inside the memo: full report citation (database, title, publisher, date) + three findings + comparison paragraph + search path
  • [ ] Filled Source Audit Form (Appendix A): ≥ 12 rows, ≥ 2 Unverified/Contradicted with actions
  • [ ] Interview bridge: 5 founder questions + 3 hypotheses, each traced to its section
  • [ ] AI reflection (below), with tools, actual prompts, and transcript links

One row per audited claim; every number in your memo needs a row; minimum 12. The verdict vocabulary is fixed — use exactly these four: Verified (two independent sources agree, method visible) · Partially verified (one credible source, or agreement with caveats) · Unverified (single source, method invisible, or the AI could not produce a checkable source) · Contradicted (credible sources disagree, or the claim collapsed on inspection). An AI summary is not a source — audit whatever it cites.

Appendix B — Sample deep-research prompts (adapt, don't copy blindly)

Prompt 1 — category scan:

You are a marketing research analyst preparing a secondary-research memo for a client that sells a prefab backyard studio (~100 sq ft, no plumbing, ~$23,000 installed) in Southern California. Produce a structured brief covering: (1) how this product category is defined and how it differs from ADUs, tiny homes, and sheds; (2) any available estimates of category size or growth in the US and California, with the publisher and year of every estimate; (3) California permitting rules for detached accessory structures under 120 sq ft vs. ADUs, citing the actual code or a government source. Rules: cite a source (name + URL) for every factual claim; if you cannot find a verifiable source, say "no reliable source found" instead of estimating; flag any source older than 3 years; do not use vendor blogs as evidence for market-level claims.

Prompt 2 — competitor & pricing matrix:

Build a comparison table of companies selling prefab backyard studios / office pods in the US (no plumbing, roughly 90–200 sq ft). Columns: company, product, floor area, advertised base price, what that price excludes (delivery, foundation, electrical, installation), estimated completed cost if reported anywhere, buying model, and warranty. Separate a second table for adjacent products that look price-comparable but are a different category (expandable container homes, foldable ADUs, travel trailers). Cite the specific product page or article for every price, with the date you accessed it. Where prices conflict between sources, show both and note the conflict.

The first output will miss exclusions and mix categories — good AI use here means you iterate, demand per-claim citations, and treat everything as unverified draft until it survives your audit.

PA3 — Define the Research Problem & Research Questions
Points 100 (term-project component)

Where this fits: Lecture — Session 1.2 Problem Definition & the Research Process (week 3) → Practice — Summit Point round r15 (Define the Research Problem — you turned Dana's discovery-call notes into objectives and RQs for a fictional client) → Now the real thing: Neo Smart Living's recorded client visit.

The gist

You translate Neo Smart Living's situation into a researchable design: the problem cascade, at least two research objectives, and at least ten typed research questions your survey will answer. Two interviews feed it — the recorded client visit and a customized GPT standing in for the founder. Done well, PA4's questionnaire writes itself: every item will trace back to an RQ you number here.

Steps

  1. Work the cascade (Appendix A): symptom → underlying problem → decision alternatives → information needs. Pick ONE decision target — existing customers or new customers — not both.
  2. Write ≥ 2 research objectives. Managerial objectives are the client's hoped-for results ("increase showroom visits"); research objectives provide information toward them ("determine which media most influence the target market's purchase behavior"). State both, clearly separated.
  3. Draft ≥ 10 research questions, numbered and grouped under the objective each serves — third person, survey-answerable, specific to the Tahoe Mini ($23K, 117 sq ft, permit-light). RQs are the manager's questions, not questionnaire questions.
  4. Label every RQ's type — Descriptive, Difference (a grouping variable + an analysis variable, one comparison per question), or Association (two continuous/ordinal variables). At least one Difference and one Association; Appendix C shows the bar.
  5. Interview the recording. Draft your full question list first (start from PA2's interview bridge), then work through the client-visit video/transcript on Canvas: log each answer with an approximate timestamp, and keep the list of questions the recording never answers — those often become RQs.
  6. Interview the GPT. Build a client persona from the client background PDF on Canvas, run the same question list you used on the recording, and keep a shareable transcript link. Note where the GPT was fluent but wrong — that's prompt (c) material.
  7. Assemble and submit: cascade + objectives + typed RQ list in the body; both interview logs (Appendix B) attached.

What to submit

  • [ ] Filled cascade (Appendix A) with ONE decision target chosen
  • [ ] ≥ 2 research objectives, each followed by its numbered RQs
  • [ ] ≥ 10 RQs, every one type-labeled; ≥ 1 Difference and ≥ 1 Association
  • [ ] Recorded-visit interview log (Appendix B): question list, answers + timestamps, unanswered list
  • [ ] GPT interview: persona prompt + shareable transcript link

At Summit Point you assembled surveys for fictional clients with instant feedback; this instrument goes in front of real respondents.

The gist

You design the questionnaire your team will actually field for Neo Smart Living: an invitation message, screeners, the items that answer your PA3 research questions, and a demographic block. The RQ → Item Map (Appendix A) is the discipline at the center — every question earns its place by answering a numbered RQ, screening, or classifying, and every RQ gets a question that truly measures it.

Steps

  1. Start from your PA3 research questions (revised per feedback). Number them; the map keys on those numbers.
  2. Draft items yourselves first — one or more questions per RQ, plus screeners (homeowner / usable outdoor space) and a demographic block at the end. Choose each scale deliberately: a 5-point-or-more Likert counts as interval data later; the two items answering your difference RQ must both be in the instrument.
  3. Fill the RQ → Item Map (Appendix A) as you go, both directions: does every question have a purpose, and does every RQ have a question that actually measures it? Fix what the Evaluation column exposes.
  4. Write the invitation message using the seven-component checklist (Appendix B). It goes on its own page, before the questionnaire.
  5. Build it in Google Forms, matching your paper draft; export to PDF.
  6. Pretest with two potential customers — friends or family who plausibly fit the target market (Path A). Note anything unclear, unanswerable, or missing, plus their interest in a showroom visit or purchase. Path B (alternative): pretest against two contrasting AI personas instead — submit the persona prompts and transcripts, plus 2–3 sentences on what an AI pretest can and cannot catch.
  7. Revise, then submit. For PA4.5, you'll revise again using instructor feedback and the class's consolidated instrument — highlight every change in green and pretest the shortened version once more.

  • [ ] Questionnaire PDF (Google Forms export): screeners → body → demographics

PA5 — Design the Sampling Plan
Points 100 (term-project component)
Due Sunday Oct 11, 11:59 PM
Work Team
Submit Canvas — one PDF: the filled fielding grid (Appendix A) + the written analysis

Where this fits: Lecture — Sessions 3.1–3.2 Sampling (week 7) → Practice — Summit Point round r10 (Select the sampling plan) → Now the real thing: the plan your team actually fields for PA5.5.

The gist

You plan exactly where, when, and from whom your team will collect its completed surveys — target: 5 × team size qualified completes. The buyer is a Southern California homeowner with usable outdoor space weighing a $23,000 backyard unit; a plan built around campus and classmates fields fast and answers nothing. The grade currency here is honesty: named places, realistic yields, and a straight account of what a convenience sample can and cannot claim.

Steps

  1. Start from the buyer, not the venue. Who plausibly spends $23K on a backyard unit? Homeowners with yards — DIY and garden-center shoppers, farmers-market regulars, outdoor-event attendees — not students between classes.
  2. Choose 4–6 named physical locations (plus at most one friends-and-family supplemental row). At least one location should be a term event from the events list on Canvas — or say explicitly why you're skipping the events.
  3. Fill the fielding grid (Appendix A): specific dates (not "flexible"), realistic time blocks, a target-segment rationale for every location, and an assigned team member on every row.
  4. Estimate completes honestly. Benchmarks: one member doing in-person intercepts completes roughly 5–15 surveys per hour in a high-traffic spot; an unpaid social post typically yields under 20 total. Plan a buffer above the 5 × team size target to absorb disqualifications.
  5. Plan motivation: the 15-second opening pitch, a product visual (tablet or printed Tahoe Mini image), a short mobile survey via QR code, and any incentive (instant thank-you + gift-card drawing).
  6. Analyze representativeness: for every location, one specific advantage for reaching the target and one specific bias risk (age, income, gender, geography, renter mix) — then ≥ 2 concrete mitigations (screener questions, per-location caps, live quota tracking).
  7. Name the method honestly: convenience sampling with a 50/50 gender quota and a homeowner / usable-outdoor-space screener — plus one sentence on why the results still cannot generalize to all SoCal homeowners.

  • [ ] Per-location estimate justifications + motivation plan

Where this fits: Lecture — Sessions 3.1–3.2 Sampling (week 7) → Practice — Summit Point round r10's fielding-QA block → Now the real thing: your own five respondents.

The gist

You personally field 5 completed, qualified surveys following your team's PA5 plan, then report how it actually went. The core of the report is four numbers kept distinct — contacts → qualified → completes → counted — and the response rate they imply. Your five join the combined pool (this class's wave + the existing student survey + the aytm n = 600 benchmark) that your PA6/PA7 analysis runs on.

Steps

  1. Use Dr. Lin's official survey link on Canvas, and ask every respondent to select your name at the end — that is how completes count toward your quota. Recruiting materials (flyer, QR code) are on Canvas.

  3. Track the four numbers as you go: contacts made (estimates OK) → qualified (passed screeners) → completes → counted toward your quota (selected your name). They are four different numbers — keep them distinct, per channel where you can.
  4. Compute your response rate = completes ÷ contacts (Appendix A shows the arithmetic).
  5. Write the report (~1 page): your method with named venues, dates/time blocks, channels, and the screener wording you used; the funnel table; then your reflection — what worked, what surprised you, and one concrete suggestion (a mechanism, not a wish) for improving the fielding process.
