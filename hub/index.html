PA2 — Secondary Research — AI Market Scan + Source AuditSun Sep 13

PA2 — Secondary Research: AI-Assisted Market Scan + Source Audit (Team)

New FA26 assignment (2026-08-05). Due Sunday Sep 13, 11:59 PM (end of week 3) — before you conduct the PA3 client interview. Professionals never walk into a client conversation cold: you do the desk research first, so your interview questions probe what the public record cannot answer. Rehearsed by simternship rounds r03 (Neo RFP) and r04 (secondary research), both due at the week-3 class.

The situation

Neo Smart Living is preparing to launch the Tahoe Mini, a prefab backyard studio at roughly $23,000 installed. Before anyone designs a survey or talks to the founder, your team builds the picture that already exists in public and library sources: what this product category is, who competes in it, what substitutes for it, what it costs elsewhere, who buys it, and what rules govern putting one in a Southern California backyard.

You will do this the modern way and the traditional way, and reconcile the two:

  • Part A — an AI deep-research pass (fast, broad, and unverified until you audit it).
  • Part B — one manual pass through the CPP Library's licensed databases (e.g., a Mintel, IBISWorld, or Statista report), the way researchers worked before AI — and the way you still verify market sizes today.
  • Part C — a source audit that decides which claims from either pass are safe to put in front of a client.

The course's standing rule applies to secondary research too: AI writes the first draft; verified sources get the final word.

Part A — AI deep research (the first draft)

Use a deep-research-capable AI tool (ChatGPT Deep Research, Gemini Deep Research, Claude, or Perplexity). Run at least two scoped research prompts and keep the full transcripts/links for your AI disclosure.

Your report must cover these areas (one section each):

  1. Category definition & size. What is the prefab backyard studio / "space pod" category? How is it different from ADUs (accessory dwelling units), tiny homes, and sheds? Any category size/growth estimates you find — with the audit treatment below; market-size numbers are where AI hallucinates most.
  2. Regulatory snapshot (California). Permitting rules for small detached backyard structures vs. ADUs in California — when is a permit required, what does the under-120-sq-ft exemption actually exempt, and why does "no plumbing" matter? This is decision-relevant: it defines what the Tahoe Mini legally is.
  3. Direct competitors. A comparison matrix of prefab backyard studio makers: product, size, advertised price vs. realistic completed cost, positioning claim, and buying model (online configurator vs. consultant-led). Minimum four direct competitors.
  4. Substitutes & adjacent categories. What else solves the same problem — room additions, garage conversions, finished shed kits, expandable/container tiny homes, coworking memberships? Note at least one price trap: a product whose price looks Tahoe-comparable but sits in a different category (kitchen/bath, permits, foundations).
  5. Customer evidence. What the public record says about who buys backyard studios and why (remote work, guest space, rental, wellness). Distinguish vendor marketing claims from independent evidence.
  6. Pricing landscape. Where does $23K installed sit in the distribution of advertised prices and completed costs?
  7. Implications → your interview plan. The bridge to PA3: five interview questions for the founder that secondary research cannot answer, and three hypotheses your primary research (focus groups, survey) should test. Each must cite the section that motivated it.

Sample deep-research prompts (adapt, don't copy blindly)

Prompt 1 — category scan:

You are a marketing research analyst preparing a secondary-research memo for a client that sells a prefab backyard studio (~100 sq ft, no plumbing, ~$23,000 installed) in Southern California. Produce a structured brief covering: (1) how this product category is defined and how it differs from ADUs, tiny homes, and sheds; (2) any available estimates of category size or growth in the US and California, with the publisher and year of every estimate; (3) California permitting rules for detached accessory structures under 120 sq ft vs. ADUs, citing the actual code or a government source. Rules: cite a source (name + URL) for every factual claim; if you cannot find a verifiable source, say "no reliable source found" instead of estimating; flag any source older than 3 years; do not use vendor blogs as evidence for market-level claims.

Prompt 2 — competitor & pricing matrix:

Build a comparison table of companies selling prefab backyard studios / office pods in the US (no plumbing, roughly 90–200 sq ft). Columns: company, product, floor area, advertised base price, what that price excludes (delivery, foundation, electrical, installation), estimated completed cost if reported anywhere, buying model, and warranty. Separate a second table for adjacent products that look price-comparable but are a different category (expandable container homes, foldable ADUs, travel trailers). Cite the specific product page or article for every price, with the date you accessed it. Where prices conflict between sources, show both and note the conflict.

What good AI use looks like here: you iterate (the first output will miss exclusions and mix categories), you demand per-claim citations, and you treat everything as unverified draft until Part C. Your AI disclosure (tool, prompts, what you verified and how) is graded.

Part B — The traditional pass: one library source, obtained manually

AI cannot access the sources professionals actually pay for. Complete one manual retrieval through the CPP University Library:

  1. Go through the library's database portal (not Google) and retrieve one licensed industry report relevant to this category — e.g., a Mintel report (home & garden / smart home / home improvement), IBISWorld (prefabricated home manufacturing, or landscaping/outdoor structures), or Statista dossier. If you're unsure which database fits, ask a librarian (chat, email, or the business liaison librarian) — note in your submission who/what you consulted and what they pointed you to.
  2. From that report, extract three findings that did not appear anywhere in your AI pass (a market size with a stated methodology, a segmentation, a trend forecast, a consumer-attitude statistic…).
  3. Write a short AI vs. library comparison (half a page): what did the licensed report have that the open web didn't? Where did the two disagree, and which do you trust for what?

Deliverable for Part B: full citation of the report (database, title, publisher, date), the three findings, the comparison paragraph, and one line on your search path (databases tried, librarian consulted if any).

Part C — The source audit (what makes your report correct)

Complete the Source Audit Form (template + worked example: PA2_Source_Audit_Form.md, posted with this assignment) for at least 12 claims, including every number that appears in your report (market sizes, growth rates, prices, percentages). For each claim you record: the source, publisher type, date, who collected it and why, the method behind the number, a corroborating second source (or the failure to find one), a verdict — Verified / Partially verified / Unverified / Contradicted — and the action you took in the report.

Two honesty rules, both graded:

  • Every number in the report body must have an audit row. A number without an audit row costs points — that's how unverified claims reach clients.
  • A clean audit is a failed audit. Real secondary research always surfaces problems — advertised prices that exclude half the cost, market sizes three sources disagree on, "category" statistics that quietly include a different category. We expect at least two claims rated Unverified or Contradicted, with what you did about them (cut the claim, ranged it, downgraded it to "vendor-claimed"). If everything verified cleanly, you didn't push hard enough.

What to submit (one PDF + one spreadsheet/table)

  1. Secondary research memo (≤ 6 pages incl. tables): sections 1–7 above, every factual claim cited (source name + link), numbers marked with their audit verdict where less than Verified (e.g., "vendor-claimed").
  2. Part B block (inside the memo): library report citation + three findings + comparison paragraph + search path.
  3. Source Audit Form (appendix or separate sheet): ≥ 12 rows.
  4. AI disclosure (course standard): tools, the actual prompts used, and how outputs were validated.

Grading (100 pts — rubric in (posted on Canvas))

Category & regulatory sections 20 · competitor/substitute analysis 20 · source audit quality 25 · library source + AI-vs-library comparison 15 · implications & interview bridge 10 · AI research process + disclosure 10. Key deductions: number without an audit row −2 each · zero Unverified/Contradicted rows −5 · price quoted without stating exclusions −2 · category confusion (ADU/tiny-home stats presented as studio-pod stats) −5.

Part 1: Research Objectives and Research Questions

Note: This assignment is highly similar to our CE1 discussion (link on Canvas).

Your project topic is typical of the kind of problem or decision facing a local business or organization. Your group's role is to act as marketing consultants to the business described by your topic.

Based on your knowledge of marketing, business principles, and the target market, you will develop research objectives and research questions that your project will answer. Your assignment should include the following elements:

Elements (1)–(4): Problem Context

(1) A general statement of the client's problem that the client hopes to solve with the information you will provide in your report.

(2) The decision(s) to be made by your client based on your research, and the decision alternatives.

(3) The target for the decision(s) — is the goal to increase satisfaction/patronage of existing customers, or is it to bring new customers to the business? You must choose ONE of these; for this project, you cannot do both.

(4) The client's managerial objectives for the research project (may overlap with 1 and 2 above).

NOTE — Managerial vs. Research Objectives: A managerial objective is what the client hopes will result from the research project. Examples: - "To develop a more effective advertising campaign" - "To increase the number of local homeowners who visit our showroom"

A research objective is different. Your research objective should be to provide information that will help your client reach the managerial objective. For example, if the managerial objective is "to develop a more effective advertising campaign," a good research objective might be "to determine which media the target audience is exposed to most frequently, and which media most strongly influence their purchase behavior."

You must provide both a managerial objective (in part 4) and research objectives (in part 5).

Element (5): Research Objectives

Your project's research objectives. You need at least two research objectives.

Some examples of well-formed research objectives:

  • Assess Southern California homeowners' awareness and knowledge of compact prefab backyard structures.
  • Identify the most effective media for a local business to use to increase awareness among its target market.
  • Determine whether potential customers prefer a particular product positioning over alternatives.
  • Identify which product features or benefits are most important to the target segment.

Element (6): Research Questions

A list of research questions for each objective in (5).

IMPORTANT: Organize this assignment so that each objective is followed by the research questions designed to meet that objective. Number the research questions so they are easy to refer to.

Research Question Types

Your research questions should include a mix of the following three types. Refer to Materials Related to Types of Research Question for definitions and examples.

1. Descriptive

Most of your research questions will be this type. You will describe characteristics of your respondents — such as how interested they are in a product, which features they prefer, or what barriers reduce their purchase likelihood.

2. Differences

In this type, you examine whether subgroups of your sample differ on some characteristic. A differences RQ has both: - A grouping variable that divides your sample into subgroups (e.g., remote vs. non-remote workers; under 45 vs. 45+; club members vs. non-members) - An analysis variable that you compare the subgroups on (e.g., purchase intention, preferred use case)

Example: "Do homeowners who work remotely differ from non-remote workers in their purchase intention for the product?"

3. Association (Relationship)

This type asks whether there is a relationship or correlation between two variables — both of which are continuous or ordinal. You are looking for whether one variable increases or decreases as the other increases.

Example: "What is the relationship between household income level and willingness to purchase the product?"

Association vs. Differences: The key distinction is that association analysis involves two continuous/ordinal variables (e.g., income level and purchase interest), while differences analysis involves splitting the sample into discrete subgroups and comparing them. Association analysis is a special case of differences analysis.

You must clearly label each research question with its type (Descriptive, Differences, or Association).

Important Reminders

  • Research questions are NOT questionnaire questions. Questionnaire questions are what you ask the consumer on a survey. Research questions are the questions the manager wants answered. Include only research questions in this assignment.

  • Research questions should not include second-person pronouns (you, your). Write them in the third person (e.g., "What proportion of homeowners..." not "How interested are you...").

  • Research questions should be very specific — tied to the client's product, price, target market, and decision alternatives. Generic questions that could apply to any business earn less credit.

  • You may include a few additional, lower-priority research questions in case there is space for them in the project.

  • Review lecture materials and CE1 discussion (link on Canvas) to make sure your research questions are phrased appropriately.

  • You can also look at example term projects (link on Canvas) to see examples of research questions.

This part has two components: a client interview based on the recorded client visit and a simulated interview using a customized GPT. You will then compare the two to reflect on the role of AI in the client discovery process.

2A. Client Interview (Recorded Visit)

Using the recorded client visit (video + transcript), your team will gather the information needed to define the marketing problem (Elements 1–4 in Part 1) and to inform your research objectives and questions.

Preparation: Complete PA2 (Secondary Research) first — your interview question list starts from PA2 Section 7 (the five questions the public record could not answer). Draft the full list before watching, then mark each question as answered (with the client's answer and an approximate timestamp) or unanswered. Your team has flexibility in how you organize and present the information. Using tables and figures is encouraged.

Key topics to cover during the interview:

  1. Product/service overview — What is the product or service being provided? What are its key features, specifications, and price point?
  2. Differentiation — What makes this product/service unique compared to alternatives? How does the client describe their competitive advantage?
  3. Competitive landscape — Who are the direct competitors? What about substitute products? (For example, if your client sells a prefab backyard unit, substitutes might include traditional room additions, ADUs, or converted sheds.)
  4. Customer profile — Who are the client's current and target customers? Describe their demographics, motivations, and usage patterns.
  5. Customer perceptions — How do different customer groups perceive the product/service? Are there common misconceptions?
  6. Research priorities — What specific questions or information does the client want from this project? How should these be prioritized?
  7. Decisions and alternatives — What decisions will the client make using this information? Get specific alternatives from the client. (For example, if the client is considering social media advertising, which platforms — Facebook, Instagram, TikTok — should they prioritize? If they are choosing between positioning concepts, which ones are they considering?)
  8. Marketing channels and partnerships — What marketing channels is the client currently using? Are there specific partnership or sponsorship opportunities being evaluated?

Sample interview questions (adapt to your client):

Category Example Question

Competitive advantage What are the foundations of your competitive advantage? What do customers say is the main reason they choose your product over alternatives?

  2. Conduct the simulated interview. Ask the GPT the same (or similar) questions you asked the real client. Cover the key topics listed in Section 2A.

Deliverable: Include the following in your submission: - Your GPT prompt/configuration (the instructions you gave the GPT) - A link to the Google Doc containing the full simulated interview transcript

2C. Comparison and Reflection

After completing both the real and simulated interviews, write a reflection (approximately 1–2 pages) addressing the following:

  1. Accuracy — How close were the simulated client's answers to the real client's answers? Where did the GPT get it right, and where did it diverge?
  2. Gaps — What information did the real client provide that the GPT could not have known or guessed? (For example, specific internal data, upcoming decisions, partnership opportunities, or personal priorities.)
  3. Strengths — In what areas was the simulated interview surprisingly useful? Did it help you prepare better questions for the real interview?
  4. Limitations — Can simulated interviews replace part of the marketing research client discovery process? Why or why not?
  5. Recommendation — Based on your experience, how would you recommend integrating AI-simulated interviews into the client discovery workflow? Before the real interview? After? As a supplement?

  6. Likert rule: treat Likert-style scales with 5 or more points as Interval (mean, SD, t-test, Pearson correlation are OK). Scales with 4 or fewer points are Ordinal (report frequency / percentage; do not report a mean).
  7. Course convention — two-way chi-square is a Difference test. When both variables are categorical, phrase the RQ as "Does [outcome] differ between [groups]?" and label it Difference. Association RQs in this class use Pearson correlation with two continuous variables only.

4. Descriptive statistic vs. statistical test

Every RQ card must specify one of these:

  • Descriptive RQ → Descriptive statistic: percent, frequency, mean, SD, median, mode. Summarizes one variable. No p-value.
  • Difference RQ → Statistical test: two-way chi-square (categorical DV) or independent-samples t-test (continuous DV). Produces a test statistic and p-value.
  • Association RQ → Statistical test: Pearson correlation (both variables continuous). Produces r and p.

"Mean" is not a statistical test. If your RQ compares means across groups, the test is an independent-samples t-test. The group means are reported alongside the t, p, and Cohen's d — but the mean itself does not test a hypothesis.

See PA6_Analysis_Test_Cheatsheet.md for the full decision table and worked examples.

Required Hand Calculations

A. Independent Samples T-Test (one Differences RQ)

Select one Differences research question where the analysis variable is interval/ratio (e.g., a mean rating). Using your raw data:

  1. State the hypotheses. Write the null hypothesis (H₀) and alternative hypothesis (H₁).
  2. Calculate group means and standard deviations for each subgroup.
  3. Compute the t-statistic using the formula covered in Chapter 12.
  4. Determine degrees of freedom (df = n₁ + n₂ − 2).
  5. Look up or calculate the p-value and compare to α = 0.05.
  6. State your conclusion in plain language: is there a statistically significant difference between groups?

Show all arithmetic steps. A table or organized layout is encouraged.

B. Two-Way Chi-Square Test (one Differences RQ)

Select one Differences research question where both variables are nominal/categorical (e.g., a crosstabulation). Using your raw data:

  1. State the hypotheses. Write H₀ (the two variables are independent) and H₁.
  2. Construct the observed frequency table (crosstab).
  3. Calculate expected frequencies for each cell: E = (row total × column total) / n.
  4. Compute the chi-square statistic (χ²) by summing (O − E)² / E across all cells.
  5. Determine degrees of freedom (df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1)).
  6. Look up or calculate the p-value and compare to α = 0.05.
  7. State your conclusion in plain language: are the two variables associated or independent?

Show all arithmetic steps including the full expected-frequency table and the cell-by-cell χ² contributions.
